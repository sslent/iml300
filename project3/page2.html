Sex ≠

Before we can ask what a woman is, we have to dismantle what we think we already know. It starts with two words people use interchangeably — but shouldn't.

01 — biological
Sex
A set of physiological characteristics — chromosomes, hormones, gonads, anatomy. Varies along a spectrum. Assigned (not chosen) at birth based on visible anatomy. More complicated than it appears.

02 — social
Gender
A social and cultural construct — the roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities that a society assigns to people. Learned, performed, enforced. Not the same as the body you inhabit.

Common myths, examined

click each to reveal
"Biology is destiny. Sex determines everything."
The relationship between biological sex and lived experience is far more complex. Hormones, social environment, culture, and individual psychology all shape who we become. Biology is one factor among many — not a fixed fate.
↓ reveal
"There are only two sexes."
Intersex conditions — variations in chromosomes, hormones, or anatomy — occur in roughly 1.7% of the population, comparable to the rate of red hair. The binary is a simplification, not a biological law.
↓ reveal
"Gender is just a feeling. Sex is the real thing."
Gender is no less real than sex — it's just real in a different register. Institutions, laws, social norms, and daily interactions are organized by gender. Its effects are entirely material.
↓ reveal
"We've always understood sex this way."
The modern biological framework for sex was largely built in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, shaped by cultural assumptions of the time. It has been revised repeatedly — and is still being revised.
↓ reveal
"XX means female. XY means male."
Chromosomal sex is more variable than the XX/XY shorthand suggests. People have XXY, XYY, X0, and other configurations. The same chromosomes can produce different phenotypes depending on gene expression and hormones.
↓ reveal
"Science has settled this question."
Science is a process, not a verdict. Feminist science scholars like Anne Fausto-Sterling have shown how cultural assumptions have shaped — and sometimes distorted — scientific inquiry into sex.

Where our ideas came from

the fly in the room

Drosophila melanogaster
common fruit fly

The science was built on flies. Not on us.

In the early 20th century, biologist Thomas Hunt Morgan conducted foundational sex chromosome research — not on humans, but on Drosophila melanogaster, the common fruit fly.

His work on X and Y chromosomes in flies became the template for how we understand biological sex in all organisms, including humans. This research earned him the Nobel Prize in 1933 — and its framework became the bedrock of modern genetics.

The problem? Flies are not humans. The way sex is determined in Drosophila differs in important ways from human sex development. The uncritical transfer of the XY model from fruit flies to people flattened the complexity of human biology — and that simplification has persisted for nearly a century.

The cursor you've been using throughout this page is a fruit fly. A small reminder that the science shaping our ideas about gender began with something much smaller — and much simpler — than us.

Chromosomal variation

hover each to learn more

Chromosomal sex is not a binary switch — it's a spectrum of variation. The chart below shows relative frequencies. Hover each column to learn more.

XX ~48%
The most common chromosomal pattern associated with female development. But chromosomes alone don't determine anatomy, hormones, or identity.
XY ~48%
The most common chromosomal pattern associated with male development. Still variable — SRY gene expression, androgen sensitivity, and other factors shape development.
XXY ~0.1%
Klinefelter syndrome. People may have any gender identity. Often not diagnosed until adulthood. Demonstrates that chromosomes are not destiny.
XYY ~0.1%
Jacob's syndrome. Usually no significant physical differences. Challenges the idea that the Y chromosome is simply "male-determining."
X0 ~0.04%
Turner syndrome. Only one X chromosome. People are typically assigned female at birth. Shows the X is not "the female chromosome."
XXX ~0.1%
Triple X syndrome. Often no symptoms. Most people live undiagnosed. A reminder that the genome is more flexible than textbooks suggest.
other ~1.7%
Intersex conditions encompass a wide range of chromosomal, gonadal, and anatomical variations — collectively affecting ~1.7% of people, comparable to the rate of natural red hair.

✦ Estimated population frequencies. Intersex variations are systematically underreported and understudied.

X

The X chromosome is not "the female chromosome."

Every human — regardless of sex — has at least one X chromosome. The X carries genes essential for life: immune function, cognition, vision, heart development. It is a chromosome of basic human biology, not a marker of femininity. The conflation of "X = female" is cultural, not scientific. It was never true to begin with.

We all run on both

hormones don't belong to a gender

Estrogen

Present and essential in all bodies — regardless of assigned sex.
In people with testes: regulates libido, bone density, and sperm maturation.
Levels fluctuate throughout life — puberty, pregnancy, menopause, aging.
Multiple forms: estradiol, estrone, estriol. Not a single thing.
· · ·

Testosterone

Present in all bodies — ovaries produce it too.
In people with ovaries: regulates mood, energy, bone mass, and libido.
Levels vary enormously between individuals — overlap between ranges is significant.
Neither a "male hormone" nor a measure of aggression, strength, or identity.
