Part II — Biology & Constructivism

Sex and gender are not the same thing.

One describes a set of physiological characteristics; the other is a social construction that varies across culture, history, and context. Understanding the difference does not diminish either — it clarifies both.

And yet even "sex," the supposedly stable biological category, turns out to be far more complicated than the XX/XY shorthand we were given in school.

What the biology actually says

Sex ≠ Gender

Sex refers to physiological characteristics: chromosomes, gonads, hormones, anatomy. Gender is the social, cultural, and psychological framework built around those characteristics — and it varies enormously across human societies. Conflating them is a category error that has shaped centuries of bad science.

The fly problem

The foundational research on sex chromosomes was conducted on Drosophila melanogaster — fruit flies. Thomas Hunt Morgan's early 20th-century work was extrapolated wholesale onto human biology. Many of its assumptions remain embedded in mainstream science despite decades of contrary evidence from human genetics and endocrinology.

Chromosomal variation

Intersex conditions demonstrate that chromosomal sex is not binary. Variations include XXY (Klinefelter), XYY, X0 (Turner), XXX, and many others. Estimates suggest 1–2% of the population is intersex — roughly as common as red hair. The binary was never the full picture; it was a simplification.

The X chromosome

The X chromosome is not "the female chromosome." All humans carry at least one X. It contains over 800 genes governing everything from colour vision to immune function. Its association with "femaleness" is a cultural overlay — the X is simply larger and gene-rich compared to the Y, which is small and mostly concerned with sex determination.

Hormones are shared

Both oestrogen and testosterone are present and necessary in all bodies regardless of assigned sex. Testosterone is produced in ovaries as well as testes. Oestrogen is essential for bone density, cardiovascular health, and neurological function in all genders. "Male" and "female" hormones is a misnomer — they are human hormones that occur in different concentrations.

Womanhood ≠ Uterus

We cannot define a woman as someone who produces eggs. Ovulation is affected by age, illness, endocrine conditions, surgical history, and more. Menopause ends egg production; this does not end womanhood. A definition that excludes post-menopausal women, women with PCOS or primary ovarian insufficiency, or women who have had hysterectomies, is not a definition — it is a gatekeeping mechanism.

So, what is a woman?

The biology is complicated. The philosophy is contested. The politics are ongoing. What these three thinkers share is a refusal to accept that the category is fixed, natural, or neutral.

Womanhood is not something you are born with intact — it is something produced, negotiated, imposed, refused, and reclaimed.
