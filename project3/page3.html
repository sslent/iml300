Chapter 03

So, what
is a woman?

Let's take a look at three different feminist's definitions of what a "woman" is.

scroll to learn about them
01 / 03
"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

— The Second Sex, 1949

Simone de Beauvoir

1908 – 1986 · France

The Second Sex (Le Deuxième Sexe)

De Beauvoir's most famous sentence is also one of philosophy's most radical. If we were innately women — if womanhood were simply a biological fact — why would we talk about it as something we have to become? Why would societies spend so much energy teaching girls how to be women?

She argued womanhood is not a nature but a situation. It is a set of roles, behaviors, and constraints that a patriarchal society imposes on people born female. "Woman" is defined not on her own terms, but in relation to man — as the Other, the second, the lack.

To become a woman is to be shaped by institutions, expectations, and myths that serve someone else's interests. Liberation begins with recognizing that this shaping is not inevitable — it is historical, contingent, and changeable.

womanhood as becoming
02 / 03
"Lesbians are not women — because 'woman' only makes sense in relation to man."

— The Straight Mind, 1980

Philosopher II

Monique Wittig

1935 – 2003 · France / United States

Key text

The Straight Mind and Other Essays

Wittig took de Beauvoir's insight further — and somewhere more unsettling. If "woman" is defined by her relationship to man, then what happens to those who refuse that relationship entirely?

For Wittig, the categories "woman" and "man" are not biological but political classes produced by a heterosexual social order. The idea that there are naturally two sexes is a myth that serves to naturalize women's subjugation. Gender, in this view, is an imaginary formation — a regime, not a reality.

Lesbians, she argued, escape the category of "woman" because womanhood is defined entirely by its sexual relationship to manhood. This is a provocation designed to expose the seams of a supposedly natural category — and ask who benefits from keeping it intact.

gender as political fiction
03 / 03
"If you free those at the bottom of the pyramid, you free everyone."

— The Transfeminist Manifesto, 2001

Philosopher III

Emi Koyama

b. 1975 · Japan / United States

Key text

The Transfeminist Manifesto

Koyama's manifesto insists that feminism's liberation project is only complete when it includes those who have been most harmed by gender norms — trans women, intersex people, and anyone who has been punished for refusing the body or role assigned to them.

For Koyama, the boundaries and binaries of gender are not natural — they are drawn out of convenience. Gender should be fluid. Bodily autonomy and gender expression are not privileges to be granted selectively; they are the foundation of freedom for everyone.

All women, she argues, must stand in solidarity with each other. The hierarchy within feminism — which women count, whose experiences are centered — is itself a tool of patriarchal control. Transfeminism is a call to dismantle the pyramid entirely, not just climb it.

solidarity across all 'women'

✦ Three answers.

A recap:

de Beauvoir

Womanhood is made, not born.

If it can be made, it can be unmade — or remade on different terms.

Wittig

The category itself is political.

The question is not who fits the category, but who the category serves.

Koyama

Liberation must be for all women.

Any feminism that leaves people behind is not truly about liberation.

Together, these thinkers don't give us a definition of woman — they give us something more useful: a set of tools for questioning the definitions we already carry.
