It's hard to answer such an enormous question, one that many people have been trying to answer for a long time. It helps if we look at some of the theory out there. Here are some of my favorite perspectives:

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

— The Second Sex, 1949

Simone de Beauvoir

1908 – 1986 · FRANCE

The Second Sex (Le Deuxième Sexe)

If we were innately women — if womanhood were simply a biological fact — why would we talk about it as something we have to become? Why would we spend so much energy teaching girls how to be "women"?

Beauvoir argued womanhood is not something innate but rather, a situation, a social condition. It is a set of roles, behaviors, and constraints that a patriarchal society imposes on people born female. "Woman" is defined not on her own terms, but in relation to man — as the Other, the second, the lack.

I'm not sure when I became a "woman", just that one day people started referring to me as a "young lady". I remember being told not to yell, not to run, and to sit with my legs closed. They said it wasn't womanly.

"'Woman' has meaning only in heterosexual systems of thought and heterosexual, economic systems. Lesbians are not women."

— The Straight Mind, 1980

Monique Wittig

1935 – 2003 · FRANCE / UNITED STATES

The Straight Mind and Other Essays

If "woman" is defined by her relationship to man, then what happens to those who refuse that relationship entirely?

For Wittig, the categories "woman" and "man" are not biological but political classes produced by a heterosexual social order. The idea that there are naturally two sexes is a myth that serves to naturalize women's subjugation. Gender, in this view, is an imaginary formation — a regime, not a reality.

Lesbians, she argued, escape the category of "woman" because womanhood is defined entirely by its sexual relationship to manhood.

As a child I remember secretly praying every night that I would never get my period. I did not have the words yet to understand my queerness, but I understood that to "become" a woman meant to have a biological and sexual contract with men.

My body, my freedom, no longer belonged to me. Looking back, I now understand my anguish politically, and identifying as a lesbian, I've spent much of my adult life untangling heterosexuality from what being a woman means to me. This is not to say that I reject the category completely, as Wittig does, but that I'd like to make it a home of my own.

"We need to acknowledge the limitation of our feminist consciousness and ethics, and pursue structural remedies to hold ourselves accountable to each other as women and as fellow human beings."

— The Transfeminist Manifesto, 2001

Emi Koyama

B. 1975 · JAPAN / UNITED STATES

The Transfeminist Manifesto

Koyama insists that feminism's goal of liberation is only complete when it includes those who have been most harmed by gender norms — trans women, intersex people, and anyone who has been punished for refusing the body or role assigned to them.

For Koyama, the boundaries and binaries of gender are not natural — they are drawn out of convenience. Gender should be fluid. Bodily autonomy and gender expression are not privileges to be granted selectively; they are the foundation of freedom for everyone.
